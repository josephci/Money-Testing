20V MAX vs 18V: same battery, different sticker

A DeWalt 20V MAX pack and a Milwaukee M18 pack hold the same cells, at the same voltage, wired the same way. The labels differ because the two companies chose to print different moments in the discharge curve. Neither is lying. Neither is stronger.

The number that separates them is not a fact about the battery. It is a choice about which fact to advertise.

Where 20 and 18 both come from

Both packs are five lithium-ion cells in series. A lithium-ion cell has two voltages worth naming:

Per cell× 5 cellsWho prints it
4.0V — fully charged, no load 20V DeWalt ("20V MAX"), Craftsman ("V20")
3.6V — nominal, the working average 18V Milwaukee (M18), Makita (LXT), Bosch, Ryobi

A 20V MAX pack reads 20V for the few seconds after it leaves the charger. Pull a trigger and it settles to about 18V, where it spends effectively its entire working life. An M18 pack does exactly the same thing — Milwaukee just named it after the part that lasts.

Outside the United States, DeWalt sells the identical pack as 18V. Other markets require the nominal figure on the label. Same cells, same tool, same performance, different regulation.

So which one is more powerful?

Neither. If two tools have the same motor and the same gearing, a 20V MAX pack and an 18V pack drive them identically. Torque comes from the motor design, the gearbox and how much current the pack can deliver — not from the number silk-screened on the side.

This matters commercially because the two numbers sit next to each other on a shelf and one of them looks bigger.

The part other explainers stop before

Almost every article about this ends at "it's just marketing." That is true, and it is not useful yet. The practical question is the next one:

If the voltage label is meaningless, what do you compare?

Why "$10 per amp-hour" breaks

The common rule of thumb in tool forums is that a battery should cost around $10 per amp-hour. It is a reasonable instinct — buyers want a normalised number — but amp-hours are the wrong unit, and the rule quietly fails the moment two packs run at different voltages.

An amp-hour measures charge, not energy. How much work that charge can do depends on the voltage pushing it. Compare a 12V-class pack with an 18V-class pack on amp-hours and you will reliably pick the worse one:

PackAhNominal VEnergy
Milwaukee M12 6.0Ah6.010.8V64.8Wh
Milwaukee M18 5.0Ah5.018V90Wh

The M12 pack has more amp-hours and 39% less energy. Judged on Ah it looks like the better buy. It is not close.

Watt-hours fold the voltage back in

Wh = nominal volts × Ah

That single multiplication makes every pack from every brand at every voltage directly comparable. Divide by price and you get the only figure that settles an argument:

$/Wh = price ÷ (Wh × number of packs)

Worked example

A DeWalt 20V MAX 5.0Ah pack. The sticker says 20V and 5.0Ah, and neither number goes into the calculation as printed:

  • Nominal voltage is 18V, not the advertised 20V
  • 18V × 5.0Ah = 90Wh — and DeWalt's own spec sheet says 90Wh, because manufacturers rate energy at nominal
  • At $109 that is $1.211 per watt-hour

The manufacturer already agrees with the arithmetic. It is only the marketing that rounds up.

Three things this changes when you buy

  1. Ignore the voltage on the box. 20V MAX, V20 and M18 are one class of battery. 12V MAX and M12 are another. Nothing separates the members of a class.
  2. Multi-packs are usually the cheapest energy. A two-pack rarely costs twice a single, so the $/Wh drops sharply. It is the most reliable discount in the category.
  3. The biggest pack is rarely the best value. Flagship high-capacity packs carry a premium per watt-hour. Two mid-size packs routinely beat one large one on both cost and total energy.
